# Classic System Design Problems

System design interviews are different from coding interviews. There's no hidden test case to pass, no single correct answer. What the interviewer is evaluating is your ability to take a vague problem ("design Twitter") and systematically break it down into concrete components, make reasoned trade-off decisions, and communicate your thinking clearly under time pressure.

The engineers who do this well aren't reciting memorized architectures. They're applying a repeatable thinking framework to every problem they encounter. This lesson teaches you that framework, then shows you how to apply it to three canonical problems — in enough detail that you could use them as templates for similar problems you encounter.

## Theory & Fundamentals

### The Interview Framework — A Repeatable Approach

Every system design interview follows roughly the same 45-minute structure. The engineers who do well are the ones who control the pacing — they know what phase they're in, how much time it deserves, and how to move between phases smoothly.

Here's the framework:

---

**Step 1: Clarify Requirements (5 minutes)**

This is the most important step, and the one most candidates skip. Jumping straight to architecture without understanding the scale and constraints is a common mistake that leads to wildly wrong designs.

Ask the following questions for every problem, without exception:

- **Scale**: How many daily active users (DAU)? How many requests per second at peak? How many users total?
- **Read/write ratio**: Is this read-heavy (news feed), write-heavy (logging), or balanced?
- **Consistency requirements**: Must a user immediately see their own writes? Can different users see slightly different states (eventual consistency)?
- **Availability requirements**: Is 99.9% uptime acceptable, or do we need 99.99%? (The difference is ~9 hours of downtime per year vs. ~1 hour.)
- **Feature scope**: Are custom domains, analytics, expiration, or search in scope? You can't design everything in 45 minutes — establish what's in scope and what's "future work."
- **Latency requirements**: Does this need p99 < 100ms, or is 1 second acceptable?

Why does this matter? A system that handles 1,000 requests/second is architecturally completely different from one that handles 1,000,000 requests/second. The first fits on a single server; the second needs a distributed architecture. Spending 30 minutes designing a highly distributed system and then learning the scale is "about 100 users" is wasted time. Spending 30 minutes designing a simple single-server architecture and then learning the scale is 100M DAU is equally wasted.

---

**Step 2: Capacity Estimation (5 minutes)**

Back-of-envelope math reveals the actual bottleneck. Don't memorize formulas — reason from numbers you know.

Useful numbers to have in your head:
- 1 day = 86,400 seconds ≈ 100,000 seconds (convenient approximation)
- 1 month ≈ 2.5 million seconds
- Average read request ≈ 1KB of data transferred
- Average user photo ≈ 1MB; average thumbnail ≈ 50KB
- A single server can handle approximately 10,000 HTTP requests/second

The standard estimates to produce:
1. **Requests per second**: divide daily requests by 100,000 (seconds in a day). Multiply by 3-5x for peak load.
2. **Storage per day/year**: (writes per day) × (average size per write)
3. **Bandwidth**: (requests per second) × (average response size)

These numbers help you immediately spot the bottleneck. A read-heavy system with 100,000 reads/second needs caching. A write-heavy system with 10,000 writes/second needs a primary-replica setup or sharding. A system storing 10TB per year needs serious thought about storage architecture.

---

**Step 3: High-Level Design (10 minutes)**

Draw the main components and data flow. Don't dive into implementation details yet. The goal is to sketch the architecture and verify it actually works at the scale you estimated.

The standard components:
- **Client** (web browser, mobile app)
- **Load balancer** (distributes traffic across servers)
- **API servers** (stateless, horizontally scalable)
- **Database** (primary + replicas)
- **Cache** (Redis, sits in front of the database)
- **Message queue** (for async work)
- **Object storage** (S3 for files/images)
- **CDN** (for static assets and cached responses)

Don't include a component unless it solves a specific problem you've identified. "I'll add Kafka because it seems scalable" is not a design decision. "I need a message queue here because the email sending is slow and I want to decouple it from the request path" is a design decision.

---

**Step 4: Deep Dive (20 minutes)**

This is where you spend most of your time. Pick 1-2 components and go deep on them. The right components to deep-dive are:
- The component that solves the hardest problem (the bottleneck you identified in estimation)
- The component the interviewer asks about

For a URL shortener, the deep dive is the code generation algorithm and the caching layer. For a rate limiter, it's the distributed counter implementation. For a news feed, it's the fan-out strategy.

In this phase, the interviewer often redirects you: "tell me more about how you'd generate the short codes" or "how does the caching work?" Follow their lead — they're signaling what they want to evaluate.

---

**Step 5: Identify Bottlenecks and Trade-offs (5 minutes)**

End by identifying where your design would break and how you'd scale it. This demonstrates systems thinking — the ability to reason about failure modes before they happen.

Ask yourself:
- What happens if this component goes down? (single points of failure)
- What happens at 10x current traffic?
- What data could be lost in a crash, and is that acceptable?
- What's the slowest operation in the critical path?

Explicitly naming trade-offs — "I chose eventual consistency here, which means a user might see their post count update with a 2-second delay, which I think is acceptable for a social app" — shows mature engineering judgment. Every design decision has a cost. Articulating those costs is what separates senior-level thinking.

---

**What NOT to do**

- Don't start drawing boxes before clarifying requirements
- Don't spend 20 minutes on a component the interviewer hasn't asked about
- Don't silently deliberate — narrate your thinking out loud even when you're uncertain
- Don't claim a design is "perfect" — nothing is, and experienced interviewers know it
- Don't say "I'll just use microservices" without explaining which services and why they're separated

## Core Patterns / Techniques

### Problem 1: Design a URL Shortener (like bit.ly)

A URL shortener is a classic first design problem. It's simple enough to cover in a single interview, but has just enough complexity to reveal how candidates think about code generation, caching, and read-heavy scaling.

---

**Step 1: Clarify Requirements**

Ask these questions:
- How many shortened URLs are created per day? (let's say 100M total stored, 1M new per day)
- How many redirects per day? (let's say 10 billion)
- Do we need custom aliases? (let's say yes, optional feature)
- Do links expire? (let's say yes, optional TTL)
- Do we need click analytics? (yes, total clicks per URL)
- Are we optimizing for read or write? (mostly reads — redirects vastly outnumber creation)

Establish scope: core features are shorten URL, redirect, and click tracking. Custom domains are out of scope for this interview.

---

**Step 2: Capacity Estimation**

```
Writes (URL creation):
  1M new URLs per day
  1M / 100,000 = 10 writes/second (very manageable)

Reads (redirects):
  10B redirects per day
  10B / 100,000 = 100,000 reads/second  ← this is the bottleneck

Storage:
  100M URLs × ~500 bytes per URL = 50GB total
  (very manageable — fits on a single machine)

Bandwidth:
  Reads: 100,000/sec × 1KB per response ≈ 100MB/sec outbound
  (manageable, but need CDN or caching to avoid all hitting the DB)
```

Key insight from estimation: this is a **heavily read-dominated system**. 100,000 reads/second with only 10 writes/second means a read:write ratio of 10,000:1. The design must optimize for reads — caching is essential.

---

**Step 3: High-Level Design**

```
  Client
    │
    ▼
  Load Balancer
    │
    ▼
  API Servers (stateless, horizontally scalable)
    │
    ├──▶ Redis Cache ──▶ Cache hit: return long URL immediately
    │         │
    │     Cache miss
    │         │
    └──────── ▼
           PostgreSQL Database
           (primary + read replicas)
```

Two main API endpoints:
- `POST /shorten` — create a short URL (10 writes/second)
- `GET /{code}` — redirect to long URL (100,000 reads/second)

The redirect endpoint is the hot path. It must be as fast as possible.

---

**Step 4: Deep Dive — Code Generation**

This is the interesting part. How do you generate a short, unique code for each URL?

**Option A: Hash the long URL**

Take the MD5 or SHA256 hash of the long URL and use the first 6-7 characters:
```
MD5("https://example.com/very/long/url") → "5d41402a..."
Short code: "5d41402"
```

Problem 1: **Hash collisions**. Two different long URLs might produce the same first 6 characters. You need a collision check: compute the code, check if it exists, if yes try the next 6 characters or add a salt and re-hash.

Problem 2: **The same URL gets a different code every time** unless you check for it. If user A and user B both shorten `https://google.com`, do they get the same short code? Probably yes (deduplication saves storage). But this requires querying by long URL — you need an index on `original_url`, not just on `short_code`.

**Option B: Counter + Base62 encoding**

Maintain a global counter. Each new URL gets the next counter value, encoded in base62.

Base62 uses characters: `a-z` (26) + `A-Z` (26) + `0-9` (10) = 62 characters. A 6-character base62 code gives 62^6 ≈ 56 billion unique codes — more than enough.

```
Counter value: 1         → base62 → "000001"
Counter value: 1,000,000 → base62 → "4c92"
Counter value: 56B       → base62 → "ZZZZZZ"
```

Pros: guaranteed uniqueness (no collisions), no extra lookups needed, simple.

Cons: codes are predictable and enumerable — someone can iterate through "000001", "000002", etc. and see all shortened URLs. This is a security concern if any URLs should be private.

**Option C: Random code with collision checking (recommended)**

Generate a random 6-character alphanumeric code. Check if it exists in the database. If it does (collision), generate another. Store it.

```
short_code = random.alphanumeric(6)  # e.g., "xK9mPq"
while exists_in_db(short_code):
    short_code = random.alphanumeric(6)
store(short_code, long_url)
```

Collision probability: with 62^6 ≈ 56 billion possible codes and 100M stored URLs, the probability of a collision on any given generation is about 100M/56B ≈ 0.2%. Low, but requires the collision check.

**Which to choose?** For an interview, present all three and recommend random generation with collision checking for its simplicity and unpredictability. Mention that counter + base62 is valid if URL enumeration isn't a concern.

---

**Step 4: Deep Dive — Caching Strategy**

100,000 reads/second cannot all hit the database. Caching is mandatory.

The access pattern is ideal for caching: the most popular URLs (link.ly/trending-meme) get millions of hits. If we cache the top 20% of URLs, we likely handle 80%+ of traffic from cache.

Cache-aside pattern:
```
GET /{code}:
  1. Check Redis for key = code
  2. HIT: return original_url immediately (< 1ms, no DB hit)
  3. MISS: query PostgreSQL for short_code = code
           Cache result in Redis with 24h TTL
           Return 301 redirect
```

What TTL? The URL doesn't change (it's immutable once created), so a long TTL is fine. 24 hours is a safe default. For URLs with click analytics, the click count changes — but if we're ok with approximate counts, we can increment a Redis counter and sync to the database periodically.

**Cache capacity**: if the average URL entry is 500 bytes and we have 100M URLs, caching all of them would take 50GB of RAM (expensive). In practice, cache the hot URLs only. With 1GB of Redis cache, you can store ~2M entries — and if the 2M most-popular URLs account for 95% of your traffic, your cache hit rate is 95%.

---

**Step 4: Deep Dive — Database Schema**

```sql
CREATE TABLE urls (
  short_code   VARCHAR(10)   PRIMARY KEY,
  original_url TEXT          NOT NULL,
  user_id      INTEGER       REFERENCES users(id),
  created_at   TIMESTAMP     DEFAULT NOW(),
  expires_at   TIMESTAMP,
  click_count  BIGINT        DEFAULT 0
);

CREATE INDEX idx_original_url ON urls(original_url);
-- For deduplication: check if this URL was already shortened
```

The primary key is `short_code` — this is the lookup key for redirects (O(log n) B-tree lookup). The index on `original_url` enables deduplication.

---

**Step 5: Bottlenecks and Scaling**

Current design handles 100,000 reads/second with caching. To scale to 10x:
- **Add Redis replicas**: route read cache queries across multiple Redis instances
- **Add API server instances**: API servers are stateless, so horizontal scaling is straightforward
- **Add database read replicas**: for cache misses, distribute read load
- **CDN for redirects**: the redirect response (`301, Location: https://...`) can be cached at CDN edge nodes closest to users, reducing latency from 50ms to 5ms for popular links

Single point of failure: the Redis cache. If Redis goes down, all requests hit the database — which would collapse under 100,000 requests/second. Mitigation: Redis Cluster with multiple nodes and replicas. Or: implement circuit breaker logic that falls back gracefully even with degraded cache.

---

### Problem 2: Design a Rate Limiter

Rate limiting is an interesting problem because it seems simple (just count requests) but the distributed nature makes it surprisingly tricky. Every API server needs access to a shared, real-time counter. How do you do that correctly and efficiently?

---

**Step 1: Clarify Requirements**

- What is being rate limited? Per user? Per IP? Per API key?
- What's the limit? (e.g., 100 requests per minute per user)
- How many users? (e.g., 1 million users actively making requests)
- How many API servers? (e.g., 10 servers)
- Is the rate limiter in the API gateway, or in each service?
- What happens when a user is rate limited? (Return 429 Too Many Requests)
- Is "sliding window" or "fixed window" rate limiting required?

Establish scope: per-user, per-minute limit, implemented in the API gateway so it applies to all services.

---

**Step 2: Why Distributed is Hard**

This is the core challenge. Let's understand why you can't just use an in-memory counter on each server.

Suppose you have 10 API servers and a limit of 100 requests/minute per user. If you store a counter on each server individually:
- User sends 100 requests, distributed across 10 servers
- Each server sees only 10 requests
- Each server thinks the user is well under the limit
- The user actually sent 100 requests but wasn't rate limited at all

The counter must be **shared** across all servers. The only way to achieve this is with a centralized store — and Redis is the right tool for this.

---

**Step 3: High-Level Design**

```
  Client
    │
    ▼
  Load Balancer
    │
    ▼
  API Servers (10 servers)
    │
    ▼ (on every request, before processing)
  Redis (shared, single-threaded = atomic operations)
    │
    ├── UNDER LIMIT: allow request through
    └── OVER LIMIT: return 429 immediately
```

Redis is perfect here because it's **single-threaded**: all operations execute serially, so incrementing a counter is atomic — there's no race condition where two servers simultaneously read "99" and both decide to let their request through.

---

**Step 4: Deep Dive — Fixed Window Algorithm**

```
Key format: "ratelimit:{userId}:{current_minute}"
Example key: "ratelimit:user123:2024-01-15-14:30"

On each request:
  count = INCR "ratelimit:{userId}:{minute}"
  if count == 1:
    EXPIRE "ratelimit:{userId}:{minute}" 60  # Key expires after 60 seconds
  if count > 100:
    return 429 Too Many Requests
  else:
    allow request
```

Why is this correct? Redis `INCR` is atomic — two simultaneous requests on the same key won't both see "100" and both be allowed through. The first to hit 101 gets 429; all subsequent get 429.

Why delete the key after 60 seconds? So the counter resets every minute. Without expiry, a user who hits their limit at minute 1 would be permanently blocked.

**The problem with fixed windows: boundary bursts**

Fixed windows have a subtle vulnerability. Suppose the window resets at exactly :00 each minute. A user can send:
- 100 requests at minute 1:59 (last second of window 1)
- 100 requests at 2:00 (first second of window 2)

Both windows allowed 100 requests. But in the 2-second window from 1:59 to 2:01, the user sent 200 requests. They successfully sent 2x the intended rate by exploiting the window boundary.

---

**Step 4: Deep Dive — Sliding Window Algorithm**

A sliding window fixes the boundary burst problem. Instead of a fixed per-minute window, you track all requests in the last 60 seconds, relative to right now.

```
At time T, count requests where timestamp > T - 60 seconds
If count >= 100: reject
```

Implementation with a Redis sorted set:
```
Key: "requests:{userId}"

On each request at timestamp T:
  ZREMRANGEBYSCORE key 0 (T - 60)  # Remove entries older than 60s
  ZCARD key                         # Count remaining entries
  if count >= 100:
    return 429
  ZADD key T T                      # Add this request's timestamp as both score and member
  EXPIRE key 60
```

The sorted set stores request timestamps as scores. We trim old entries on every request, then count what's left. If under the limit, add this request's timestamp and allow it.

This is more accurate but uses more memory (storing individual timestamps) and has more Redis operations per request. For most practical systems, fixed window is a good enough approximation.

---

**Step 4: Deep Dive — Token Bucket Algorithm**

Token bucket is a third approach that allows short bursts while enforcing an average rate.

Imagine a bucket that:
- Holds at most N tokens (N = burst capacity)
- Refills at a rate of R tokens per second (R = sustained rate)
- Each request consumes 1 token
- If the bucket is empty, reject the request

A user who hasn't made requests in a while accumulates tokens (up to the max). They can then burst at the max rate before being throttled to the refill rate. This is more user-friendly than strict per-second limits.

Redis implementation: store `{tokens, last_refill_time}` per user. On each request:
1. Calculate how much time has passed since `last_refill_time`
2. Multiply by the refill rate to get tokens to add
3. Cap at the bucket maximum
4. If tokens >= 1, deduct 1 and allow; else reject

This requires multiple Redis operations (read, compute, write), so it must be wrapped in a Redis transaction or Lua script to prevent race conditions.

---

**Step 5: Bottlenecks and Scaling**

The rate limiter adds latency to every request. A Redis round-trip is typically 0.5-2ms. This is acceptable for an API gateway, but measure and set a timeout — if Redis is slow, you need to decide: fail open (allow all requests when Redis is slow, risking abuse) or fail closed (reject all requests when Redis is slow, breaking the application).

For most applications, **fail open** is better — a brief period without rate limiting is less bad than a brief period where all requests fail.

Scaling Redis: with 1 million active users, each storing a counter key, the memory requirement is tiny (a few bytes per key). A single Redis instance can handle hundreds of thousands of operations per second. For extreme scale, Redis Cluster shards the key space across multiple nodes.

### Problem 3: Design a News Feed (like Twitter/Instagram)

The news feed problem is the canonical example of a system where the right answer depends entirely on your access patterns, and there's a genuine trade-off with no perfect solution.

---

**Step 1: Clarify Requirements**

- What's the scale? (let's say 10M DAU, 100M total users)
- Can a user follow anyone? Are there follower limits? (no limits — celebrities can have millions)
- What appears in the feed? (posts from users you follow, sorted newest first)
- Do we need to handle images/videos? (yes, but treat them as opaque blobs)
- How deep can users scroll? (paginate — let's say 50 posts per page, max 500 posts in feed)
- Is real-time feed refresh required? (near-real-time, within 1-2 seconds of a post)
- How many followers can a user have? (up to millions — the celebrity problem)

Key constraint to identify: **celebrity users** with millions of followers require special handling. This is the core design challenge.

---

**Step 2: Capacity Estimation**

```
Users: 10M DAU, 100M total
Posts: assume 1M posts/day (10 writes/second)
Feed reads: each DAU loads feed 5 times/day = 50M feed loads/day
Feed reads per second: 50M / 100,000 = 500 reads/second at average
Peak: 500 × 3 = 1,500 reads/second at peak

Post content storage: 1M posts/day × 500 bytes = 500MB/day = 180GB/year
Media storage: 1M posts × some fraction have images → handled by S3, not our concern for the feed itself
```

500-1,500 feed reads/second is manageable with good caching. The 10 writes/second for posts is tiny. This system's challenge is not raw throughput — it's the **fan-out problem**.

---

**Step 3: High-Level Design**

Core data model:
```sql
-- Who posted what
posts (id, user_id, content, media_url, created_at)

-- Who follows whom
follows (follower_id, followee_id, created_at)
  index on (follower_id)   -- "who does user X follow"
  index on (followee_id)   -- "who follows user X"

-- Pre-computed feed for each user (the feed cache)
feed_items (user_id, post_id, created_at)
  index on (user_id, created_at DESC)   -- "get feed for user X, newest first"
```

The `feed_items` table is the key design decision. Let's discuss why it exists.

---

**Step 4: Deep Dive — Fan-Out on Write vs. Fan-Out on Read**

This is the central architectural question of the news feed problem. When user A posts, how do their followers see it?

**Approach 1: Fan-Out on Read (pull model)**

When user B loads their feed, the system computes it:
1. Query: "give me all users that B follows"
2. For each followed user: "give me their N most recent posts"
3. Merge all results, sort by time, return first 50

```sql
SELECT p.* FROM posts p
JOIN follows f ON f.followee_id = p.user_id
WHERE f.follower_id = :user_b_id
ORDER BY p.created_at DESC
LIMIT 50
```

**Advantages**: writes are instant (just store the post once). No extra work per post. Storage is minimal — just one copy of each post.

**Disadvantages**: every feed read queries across all the users B follows. If B follows 500 people, this is a complex query touching many rows. At 1,500 feed reads/second, this puts enormous read load on the database. Adding an index on `(user_id, created_at)` helps, but still requires merging 500 result sets. At scale, this breaks.

**Approach 2: Fan-Out on Write (push model)**

When user A posts:
1. Store the post in the posts table
2. Look up all of A's followers
3. For each follower, insert a row in their `feed_items` table: "user B's feed should include post P"

```
User A posts → background worker:
  for each follower of A:
    INSERT INTO feed_items (user_id, post_id, created_at)
    VALUES (follower_id, new_post_id, NOW())
```

When user B loads their feed, it's just:
```sql
SELECT p.* FROM feed_items fi
JOIN posts p ON p.id = fi.post_id
WHERE fi.user_id = :user_b_id
ORDER BY fi.created_at DESC
LIMIT 50
```

**Advantages**: feed reads are extremely fast — the feed is pre-computed. The read query is simple and fast.

**Disadvantages**: when user A posts, you must write to every follower's feed. If A has 10,000 followers, that's 10,000 inserts for one post. If A has 1 million followers (celebrity), that's 1 million inserts — potentially taking minutes.

This is the **celebrity problem** or **hot write** problem.

**Approach 3: Hybrid (the real answer)**

The hybrid approach routes differently based on the poster's follower count:

```
When user A posts:
  if A.follower_count <= THRESHOLD (e.g., 10,000):
    fan-out on write: push to all followers' feed_items
  else:
    don't fan-out: just store the post
    (celebrities are handled on read)

When user B loads their feed:
  1. Fetch B's pre-computed feed from feed_items
     (fast — covers all non-celebrity follows)
  2. For each celebrity B follows (followee.follower_count > THRESHOLD):
     fetch their recent posts
  3. Merge celebrity posts with pre-computed feed
  4. Sort and return
```

Celebrity posts are fetched on read, but they're cached aggressively — Taylor Swift's last 50 posts, cached for 1 minute, serve all 50M fans who follow her.

This hybrid approach is how Twitter, Instagram, and most large social networks actually work. It's the answer to "how do you handle celebrities in a news feed?" that interviewers are looking for.

---

**Step 4: Deep Dive — Storing the Feed Cache in Redis**

The `feed_items` table gets queried constantly. To make feed reads as fast as possible, cache each user's feed in Redis using a sorted set:

```
Key: "feed:{user_id}"
Sorted set: score = post timestamp (Unix epoch), member = post_id

When user B loads their feed:
  ZREVRANGE "feed:{user_b_id}" 0 49  # Get 50 most recent post IDs

When user A (non-celebrity) posts:
  for each follower_id of A:
    ZADD "feed:{follower_id}" timestamp post_id
    ZREMRANGEBYRANK "feed:{follower_id}" 0 -501  # Keep only 500 most recent
```

Feed reads become a single Redis command. The database query is only needed if the user's feed is not in Redis (cold start), or for pagination beyond the cached 500 posts.

---

**Step 4: Deep Dive — Pagination**

Never load an entire feed at once. Use cursor-based pagination:

```
First request: GET /feed?limit=50
Response: { posts: [...], next_cursor: "2024-01-15T14:30:00Z:post_id_123" }

Next page: GET /feed?limit=50&cursor=2024-01-15T14:30:00Z:post_id_123
Response: { posts: [...], next_cursor: "..." }
```

The cursor encodes the position in the result set (timestamp + post ID for tie-breaking). This is more reliable than offset-based pagination (`?page=2`) because:
- Offset pagination breaks when new posts are inserted: page 2 with offset 50 shifts by one for every new post added
- Cursor pagination is stable: the cursor points to a specific position, so new posts don't affect it

---

**Step 5: Bottlenecks and Scaling**

**Post storage**: 180GB/year is manageable on a single PostgreSQL instance initially. As it grows, shard by user_id.

**Feed generation for new users**: when a user signs up and follows 200 accounts, their feed cache is empty. You need a "feed hydration" job that retroactively computes their feed from recent posts by their follows. This is a one-time expensive operation, done asynchronously after they set up their follows.

**The thundering herd on celebrity posts**: when a celebrity posts, many users' feeds need to be updated simultaneously (or fetched fresh). Use background job queues to spread out the fan-out work. Don't do it synchronously.

**10x scaling path**:
- At 10x DAU (100M): add more Redis nodes for the feed cache. Shard the feed cache by user_id across Redis Cluster. Add more API server instances.
- At 100x DAU (1B): geo-distribute the entire system. US users' feeds served from US data center, EU users from EU. Each region has its own feed cache and database replica.

---

**Putting It All Together: Component Summary**

```
Architecture Overview:

 Client
   │
   ▼
 CDN (static assets, cached API responses)
   │
   ▼
 Load Balancer
   │
   ▼
 API Servers (stateless, autoscaling)
   │
   ├──▶ Redis Cluster (feed cache, rate limiting)
   │
   ├──▶ PostgreSQL (posts, follows, users)
   │         ├── Primary (writes)
   │         └── Read Replicas (complex queries)
   │
   ├──▶ Message Queue (async fan-out workers)
   │         └── Fan-out Workers (post to followers)
   │
   └──▶ Object Storage S3 (images, videos)

Background Jobs:
   Fan-out Workers: subscribe to "post.created" events
                    fan-out to followers' feed caches
```

## Best Practices

- **Clarify before designing** — the first 5 minutes determines whether you solve the right problem. Never skip requirements gathering.
- **Do the math** — back-of-envelope estimation is not optional. The numbers reveal whether you need caching, sharding, or replication. Without numbers, your design is speculation.
- **Identify the one hard thing** — every design problem has one genuinely difficult component. Spend your time on that. For URL shortener it's the caching strategy. For rate limiter it's the distributed counter. For news feed it's the fan-out. Don't describe the load balancer in detail.
- **Name trade-offs explicitly** — "I'm choosing fan-out on write, which is faster for reads but slower for high-follower writers. I'd handle celebrities with fan-out on read to avoid the celebrity problem." This demonstrates mature thinking.
- **Design for the bottleneck** — the bottleneck is always the slowest, most constrained component. Design is about identifying and solving the bottleneck, not describing all the components.
- **Show the scaling path** — end with "to handle 10x traffic, I'd do X" even if you don't have time to detail it. This shows you can think beyond the MVP.
- **Draw the diagram** — a visual component diagram is worth many words. It forces clarity about data flows and communication patterns.
- **Narrate your thinking** — even when uncertain, say "I'm thinking about this trade-off..." Silence is much worse than uncertainty.

## Common Interview Questions

**Q1: How would you handle collisions in URL shortener code generation?**
A: With random code generation, check the database before inserting: generate a candidate code, query whether it exists, insert if not. Two concurrent writers might both see "not exists" and both try to insert — resolve this with a UNIQUE constraint on `short_code`. The first writer succeeds; the second gets a unique constraint violation, catches it, generates a new code, and retries. Collision probability with 62^6 possible codes and 100M existing codes is about 0.2%, so retries are rare. Alternatively, pre-generate a pool of codes offline and distribute them atomically to API servers.

**Q2: How do you make a rate limiter that works across multiple API servers?**
A: Use Redis as a shared, centralized counter store. Redis is single-threaded, so `INCR` operations are atomic — no race conditions. Key format: `ratelimit:{userId}:{time_window}`. Each API server increments the same Redis key on every request. If the count exceeds the limit, return 429. Set a TTL on the key equal to the window duration so the counter resets automatically. This requires Redis to be highly available — use Redis Cluster or Redis Sentinel for redundancy.

**Q3: What is the celebrity problem in news feeds and how do you solve it?**
A: When a celebrity with millions of followers posts, fan-out on write requires updating millions of feed caches — potentially millions of database/cache writes from a single post. This overwhelms the system. Solution: hybrid fan-out. For users below a follower threshold (e.g., 10,000), fan-out on write as normal. For celebrities, don't fan-out — instead, when any user loads their feed, fetch celebrity posts separately on read. Celebrity posts are cached aggressively (they're read by many users), so this read path is fast.

**Q4: What is cursor-based pagination and why is it better than offset-based?**
A: Cursor-based pagination uses a pointer to a specific item (e.g., the last item seen, identified by ID and timestamp) as the starting point for the next page. Offset-based uses a numeric offset (skip 50, take 50). Cursor-based is better for feeds because: (1) it's stable — new posts added while a user is scrolling don't cause items to shift between pages, which causes duplicates or skips with offset; (2) it performs better on large datasets — offset-based pagination at deep offsets requires the database to scan and discard all preceding rows; cursor-based jumps directly to the position.

**Q5: How would you ensure the URL shortener handles 100,000 reads/second?**
A: Cache-aside with Redis is the primary tool. The redirect endpoint checks Redis first; a cache hit (< 1ms) returns the long URL with no database load. Cache the most popular URLs — even 1GB of Redis caches millions of entries. For URLs not in cache, queries hit PostgreSQL read replicas (primary is write-only). Set a long TTL on cached URLs (they're immutable). For extreme scale, add CDN caching: a 301 redirect can be cached at CDN edge nodes, so repeated requests for the same short URL never even reach your servers.

**Q6: How would you scale the news feed from 10M to 100M DAU?**
A: At 10x scale, the main pressures are: (1) feed cache size grows 10x — shard the Redis feed cache by user_id across multiple Redis Cluster nodes; (2) post database grows 10x — add read replicas and eventually shard by user_id; (3) fan-out workers must process 10x more events — scale worker count horizontally. The most important optimization: ensure your fan-out queue has enough throughput headroom. If celebrity fan-out still causes lag at 100M DAU, add a dedicated high-priority queue for non-celebrity posts (they affect everyone's feed immediately) and a low-priority queue for celebrity fan-out (eventually consistent is acceptable for celebrity posts).

**Q7: Why is Redis a good choice for the rate limiter counter, and what happens if Redis goes down?**
A: Redis is single-threaded, so operations like INCR are atomic — no race condition between two servers simultaneously reading and incrementing a counter. It's also extremely fast (sub-millisecond operations) and supports automatic key expiry (TTL), which handles window resets. If Redis goes down: you must decide whether to fail open (allow all requests, temporarily disabling rate limiting — acceptable for brief outages) or fail closed (reject all requests, which breaks your API — almost never acceptable). Fail open is the right default. Mitigate with Redis Sentinel or Redis Cluster for high availability.

**Q8: What's the trade-off between 301 and 302 redirects in a URL shortener?**
A: 301 (Moved Permanently) tells browsers and CDNs to cache the redirect — subsequent requests for the same short URL go directly to the long URL without hitting your servers. This reduces load dramatically. 302 (Found, temporary redirect) tells browsers not to cache — every redirect hits your servers. The trade-off: 301 is more efficient but means you lose click tracking (browser goes directly to destination, bypassing your analytics). 302 lets you record every click but puts load on your servers. Most URL shorteners that need click analytics use 302 (or 307 for method-preserving redirects). If analytics isn't required, 301 is strictly better.

## Practice Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Design a Pastebin (like pastebin.com)**

Users can create text snippets ("pastes") with an optional expiry date. Anyone with the link can view the paste. Scale: 10M pastes stored, 100M views/day.

Walk through: (1) requirements clarification questions, (2) capacity estimation, (3) high-level architecture, (4) code generation strategy (how is the paste ID generated?), (5) storage design (where does the text live — database or object storage?), (6) caching strategy for reads, (7) handling expiry (how do expired pastes get cleaned up?).

Hint: Very large pastes (10MB of code) shouldn't be in your relational database. Think about when to use object storage (S3) vs. a database.

**Exercise 2 — Design a Leaderboard**

A mobile game needs a global leaderboard: given any player, show their rank and the top 100 players. The game has 10M active players. Scores update in real-time as players finish matches. Leaderboard reads must be fast (< 50ms).

Walk through: (1) why a naive SQL solution fails at scale, (2) how Redis sorted sets solve this problem (ZADD, ZRANK, ZREVRANGE — what do each do and what do they return?), (3) what happens when a score update arrives — what Redis operations run?, (4) persistence — what if Redis restarts?, (5) how do you show a user their rank even if they're not in the top 100?

Hint: Redis sorted sets give you O(log n) rank queries, which is the key to this problem.

**Exercise 3 — Design a Notification System**

Multiple services (order service, social service, payment service) need to send notifications to users via three channels: push notification, email, and SMS. Each user has channel preferences (e.g., "push + email for orders; email only for social"). Services have different reliability requirements: payment notifications must be delivered; social notifications are best-effort.

Walk through: (1) the event-driven architecture for receiving events from multiple services, (2) the user preferences lookup (where is this data stored and how do you make it fast?), (3) the per-channel worker design, (4) retry and DLQ strategy — should payment notifications have a different retry policy than social notifications?, (5) deduplication — what happens if the same notification event is delivered twice?

Hint: Consider how to structure the message routing so that adding a new channel (WhatsApp) requires minimal changes.

**Exercise 4 — Design a Typeahead / Autocomplete**

A search box shows suggestions as the user types: typing "star" shows "star wars", "starbucks", "starcraft". The suggestions must appear within 100ms. There are 100M possible search terms. Terms are ranked by popularity (how often they've been searched).

Walk through: (1) the data structure that enables prefix lookups efficiently (trie? sorted index?), (2) storage: where do the search terms and their popularity scores live?, (3) caching: which terms should be cached in Redis, and what's the key structure?, (4) how do you update the popularity scores as new searches come in — synchronously or asynchronously?, (5) how does the system handle 10,000 simultaneous users typing different things?

Hint: Prefix searches are naturally hard for B-tree indexes. Think about whether pre-computing suggestions for common prefixes is feasible.

## Key Takeaways

- Every system design interview follows the same framework: clarify requirements → estimate capacity → sketch high-level design → deep dive on the hard parts → identify bottlenecks. Use this structure every time.
- Back-of-envelope estimation is not optional. The numbers reveal the architecture: 100K reads/second requires caching; 10K writes/second requires primary-replica or sharding; 50GB storage fits on one machine.
- URL shortener: core challenge is the code generation strategy and read-heavy caching. Random codes with collision checking are safe. Cache-aside in Redis handles 100K+ reads/second. 301 redirects for efficiency, 302 for analytics.
- Rate limiter: core challenge is the distributed counter. Redis INCR is atomic across all servers — this is the key insight. Fixed window is simple; sliding window is more accurate; token bucket allows bursts. Fail open when Redis is unavailable.
- News feed: core challenge is the fan-out problem. Fan-out on write (pre-computed, fast reads) vs. fan-out on read (simple writes, slow reads). Hybrid for celebrities: fan-out on write for normal users, fan-out on read for celebrities. Redis sorted sets cache per-user feeds.
- Always name trade-offs explicitly. "I chose eventual consistency here because X" is much better than silently assuming everything is consistent.
- The skill being tested is communication: can you think through a complex, ambiguous problem out loud in real time? The best technical design communicated poorly scores worse than a good-enough design communicated clearly.
