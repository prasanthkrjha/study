# Mock Practice

Knowledge and performance under interview conditions are different skills. This unit is about closing that gap deliberately — structured practice protocols that convert what you know into what you can consistently demonstrate when it counts.

## Theory & Fundamentals

### Why Mocks Work Differently Than Studying

Studying under comfortable conditions trains recognition: you can identify a sliding window problem when reading it. Mock interviews train retrieval under pressure: you can identify the pattern when a stranger is watching you type on a clock. These are different cognitive skills and require different practice.

The gap shows up most clearly in three places:
- **Blank-screen panic**: the first 30 seconds of a problem where nothing comes to mind, even though you've solved similar problems before.
- **Narration failure**: knowing what to code but being unable to explain why simultaneously.
- **Result vagueness**: knowing what happened in a project but not knowing how to quantify it on the spot.

Deliberate mock practice targets all three explicitly, not as side effects of more studying.

### The Three Interview Types and Their Practice Requirements

**DSA/Coding interviews** require:
- Pattern recognition under time pressure
- Narrating approach before coding, complexity analysis after
- Handling edge cases explicitly
- Recovering gracefully when an approach fails

**System design interviews** require:
- Asking clarifying questions before proposing solutions
- Narrating trade-offs while drawing, not just drawing
- Moving through the right phases (requirements → high-level → deep-dive) in proportion
- Managing the 45-minute clock yourself

**Behavioral interviews** require:
- Speaking in STAR structure without explicitly invoking it
- Using "I" language, not "we"
- Quantifying results spontaneously, without prompting
- Staying within 2-3 minutes without trailing off

Each type degrades in a different way under pressure and needs targeted practice.

## Core Patterns / Techniques

### Timed DSA: The Interview Simulation Protocol

Conditions that matter beyond just solving the problem:

**Environment setup**:
- Plain text editor or paper — no IDE autocomplete. Real interviews, especially on-site, give you a shared doc or whiteboard.
- A visible countdown timer: 25 minutes for a medium problem, 15 for easy, 35-40 for hard.
- No looking up syntax during the timer. Tabbing to MDN mid-problem costs you time and signals unfamiliarity.

**During the problem**:
1. Read the problem aloud. Repeat the constraint that matters most ("so the array can be up to 10^5 elements — that rules out O(n²)").
2. State your approach before writing a single line: "I think this is a two-pointer problem because we need a sum pair. Let me verify: sorted array, find two elements summing to target — yes, two pointers from both ends." Give the interviewer 15 seconds to redirect before you commit.
3. Code while narrating key decisions: "I'm using a set here for O(1) lookup" — not every line, just the decisions.
4. State time and space complexity without being asked: "This is O(n) time and O(n) space for the hash map."
5. If you run out of time: state your approach verbally and what the next steps would be. A correct algorithm in words is better than incomplete code.

**After the problem**:
Write one sentence: what slowed you down. "I didn't recognize the monotonic stack pattern for 15 minutes." That's the thing to drill next.

**Pacing benchmarks** (approximate):
- Easy: fully solved + explained within 12 minutes
- Medium: fully solved + explained within 25 minutes
- Hard: correct approach identified within 10 minutes, working code within 35-40 minutes

### System Design: The Verbal Practice Protocol

System design practice fails when it's done silently on paper. The deliverable of a system design interview is a conversation, not a diagram.

**Before every design session, ask these clarifying questions:**
- Scale: How many users? What's the read/write ratio? Requests per second?
- Consistency: Do all users need to see the same data immediately, or is eventual consistency acceptable?
- Availability: What's the uptime requirement? Can we tolerate brief downtime during a deploy?
- Latency: Is this user-facing (< 200ms) or batch (seconds acceptable)?
- Geography: Is this global, or regional?

You don't need all the answers. You need to ask the questions — it signals that you understand that system design answers depend on requirements.

**The 45-minute structure to practice:**
- 0-5 min: clarifying questions and scope
- 5-15 min: high-level design (components, data flow)
- 15-35 min: deep dive on 1-2 critical components (the examiner will direct you)
- 35-45 min: trade-offs, failure modes, scaling from 1M to 100M users

**The narration habit**: every time you draw a component or an arrow, say why:
- "I'm using a message queue here instead of a direct API call because the notification doesn't need to block the user's checkout flow."
- "I'm putting a cache in front of the database because the read/write ratio is 100:1 and most reads are for the same popular items."

This is what separates a strong candidate from one who draws a correct diagram silently — the interviewer cannot grade what you don't say.

**Common designs to practice verbally** (from Unit 5.5):
1. URL shortener
2. Rate limiter
3. Real-time chat application
4. News feed / timeline
5. Notification system

Run through each one verbally to a peer, a phone recording, or even to yourself. The first run will be messy. That's the point.

### Behavioral: The Recording Protocol

Record yourself (phone video is sufficient) answering 3-5 questions from your Unit 9.1 story bank, then watch the playback. This is uncomfortable — do it anyway.

**What to look for in playback:**
- **STAR structure clarity**: Could someone who's never heard your story understand the Situation in 1-2 sentences? Is the Action clearly yours ("I decided to...") rather than the team's ("we decided to...")?
- **Result specificity**: Does your Result end with a number, or does it trail off with "...and it went well"?
- **Filler words**: "like," "um," "you know" — they signal uncertainty and eat time.
- **Time**: Did you stay within 2-3 minutes? Longer than 3 minutes = over-explaining Situation; under 1 minute = skipping the Action.
- **Energy drop**: Many candidates start strong and trail off in the Result. Watch for the energy dropping on the most important sentence.

**Re-record until**:
- The Situation is one sentence
- The Action has specific decisions in "I" language
- The Result includes a number or concrete outcome
- The whole answer is 2-3 minutes

**The follow-up drill**: After recording a baseline answer, have a peer ask three follow-up questions:
- "What would you do differently?"
- "How did your manager/teammate respond?"
- "What was the hardest part?"

These are where candidates with bullet-point notes (Unit 9.1) outperform candidates with memorized scripts. Practice going off-script from your bullet points.

### Combined Mock Sessions

As real interviews approach, run combined sessions that simulate a full interview day:

- **30-minute DSA problem** (timed, narrated, reviewed)
- **20-minute system design** (verbal, with trade-offs)
- **10-minute behavioral** (3 questions from the story bank, recorded)

The goal is not to get each section right in isolation (you already practice that) — it's to build the context-switching stamina to perform well after switching cognitive modes three times in a row, which is what a real interview day requires.

### Platforms and Resources

- LeetCode — sort by pattern, not random; work through each DSA unit's curated list from the tracker

## Best Practices

- **One concrete retro item per session**: After every mock, write down the single most important thing to fix. Not "do better," but something specific: "I didn't ask about read/write ratio before designing" or "I went silent for 90 seconds while coding." That specificity is what compounds across sessions.
- **Time mocks from day one of the pattern — not day one of DSA**: Timing before you have pattern recognition just builds anxiety. Once you've worked through the problems in a topic and understand the pattern, start timing.
- **Practice in the same environment as the real interview**: If the interview is on CoderPad, practice on CoderPad. If it's a Google Doc, practice on a Google Doc. The tools matter — switching environments in the interview itself is a friction you don't need.
- **Simulate the behavioral round, not just the technical**: Most candidates spend 90% of prep time on DSA and system design, then show up to the behavioral round under-rehearsed. Behavioral answers should be as polished as technical explanations.
- **Identify patterns in your failures**: Keep a log. If you fail three mocks because you can't explain trade-offs in system design, that's the thing to study — not more DSA.

## Common Interview Questions About Your Process

**Q: "How did you prepare for interviews?"**
A: Three tracks: timed DSA problem-solving organized by pattern (sliding window, graph traversal, DP) rather than random practice; verbal system design sessions where I practiced narrating trade-offs while drawing, not just drawing; and recorded behavioral answers that I reviewed for STAR structure and specific results. I kept a running log of what broke in each mock and targeted those gaps in the next session.

**Q: "What's your process when you get stuck on a coding problem?"**
A: First, I narrate what I do know — "I know the output needs to be this structure, and I know the brute force would be O(n²)." Speaking it out loud usually reveals an edge I hadn't considered. If still stuck, I try a concrete small example — trace through it by hand rather than coding blindly. If I'm genuinely stuck at the 15-minute mark, I tell the interviewer: "I want to flag that I'm stuck and share what I've tried" — interviewers can often give a small hint, and asking for a hint after clearly showing your thinking is much better than sitting silently.

**Q: "How do you decide when to move on vs. go deeper in a system design?"**
A: I track the clock. In a 45-minute session, I budget: 5 minutes for requirements, 10 for high-level design, 20-25 for the deep dive, 5-10 for trade-offs. If I notice I've spent 20 minutes on requirements and haven't drawn anything, I consciously summarize and move on. Interviewers will redirect if they want more depth on something — my job is to make sure I've covered the full system, not perfected one component.

## Practice Exercises

1. **Solo DSA sprint**: Set a 25-minute timer. Open a blank file (no IDE). Solve one medium LeetCode problem of your choice. After the timer: review your solution, state the complexity out loud, then identify what slowed you down. Repeat 3x per week for one month.

2. **System design verbal run-through**: Pick URL shortener. Set a 45-minute timer. Speak out loud through the entire design as if the interviewer is in the room: ask your own clarifying questions, narrate each component as you draw it, state trade-offs explicitly. Record yourself. Watch the playback and count how many times you narrate a trade-off vs. draw in silence.

3. **Behavioral recording stack**: Record yourself answering three questions: "Tell me about a time you made a mistake," "Tell me about a technical decision you're proud of," and "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate." Watch all three. Pick the weakest Result section and re-record until the result is specific and quantified.

4. **Mock interview exchange**: Find one peer also preparing for interviews. Run one 60-minute mock per week: 30-minute DSA (one person gives a problem, the other solves it out loud), 30-minute behavioral (5 questions each). The person asking must give feedback: one thing done well, one thing to improve.

5. **Failure log review**: After 4 weeks of mocks, review your failure log. Identify the top 3 recurring failure modes. Spend one week in targeted practice on each — this is the fastest path to closing the remaining gaps.

## Key Takeaways

- Studying builds recognition; mocks build retrieval under pressure. Both are required, but they are not the same practice.
- For DSA: narrate before coding, state approach and complexity explicitly, timer visible, no autocomplete.
- For system design: clarifying questions before drawing anything, narrate trade-offs while drawing, manage the clock yourself through requirements → high-level → deep-dive → trade-offs.
- For behavioral: STAR structure, "I" language, quantified results, 2-3 minutes — record yourself and watch the playback, which reveals weaknesses that internal rehearsal doesn't.
- Combined sessions (DSA + system design + behavioral in sequence) build the context-switching stamina a real interview day requires.
- One specific retro item per session, written down, compounding — this is what makes mock practice improve over time rather than just repeat.
- Peer mocks on Pramp or Interviewing.io add realistic pressure with a stranger — practice with someone who doesn't know your stories and hasn't seen your problem-solving before.
