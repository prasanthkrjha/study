# Mock Practice

Knowledge and performance under interview conditions are different skills — this unit is about closing that gap deliberately, not about learning new material.

## Timed DSA under interview conditions

The conditions that matter, beyond just solving the problem:

- **No IDE autocomplete** — practice on a plain text editor or paper/whiteboard occasionally, since real interviews (especially onsite/whiteboard ones) don't give you IntelliSense.
- **Think out loud** — narrate your approach *before* coding: "I think this is a sliding window problem because we're looking at contiguous substrings — let me confirm the approach before I start typing." Interviewers are evaluating your reasoning process, not just the final code; silent coding gives them nothing to evaluate.
- **A real timer** — 25-30 minutes for a medium problem. Running out of time in practice, with no consequence, teaches you nothing about pacing. Running out of time with a visible clock teaches you when to cut losses on an approach that isn't working and try a different angle.
- **State your complexity** at the end without being asked — it signals fluency rather than something you had to be prompted for.

## Verbal system design

System design practice fails when it's done silently on paper. Practice specifically:

- **Asking clarifying questions first** — scale, read/write ratio, consistency requirements (Unit 5.1-5.5) — before drawing anything. Jumping straight to a diagram without clarifying requirements is one of the most common mistakes.
- **Narrating trade-offs as you draw** — "I'm choosing a queue here instead of a direct call because the user doesn't need to wait for the email to send" is a much stronger answer than silently drawing a queue.
- **Managing the clock yourself** — in a real 45-minute system design interview, you decide how much time to spend on high-level architecture versus deep-diving one component; practice noticing when you're spending too long on one part.

## Recorded behavioral answers

Record yourself (phone video is enough) answering 3-5 questions from your Unit 9.1 story bank, then actually watch the playback. This is uncomfortable but disproportionately useful — you'll notice filler words, rambling openings, or a weak "Result" that trails off, none of which are obvious from the inside while you're speaking. Re-record until the STAR structure is clear and the result is quantified where possible.

## A practice cadence that actually builds the skill

- **Early on:** untimed DSA, focused purely on pattern recognition — timing too early just builds anxiety without yet having the pattern-matching speed to justify it.
- **Once a pattern has real reps behind it:** start timing.
- **As mock interviews approach:** combine all three — timed DSA, verbal system design, and recorded behavioral — in the same session occasionally, since real interview days involve context-switching between rounds without a break to mentally reset.
- **Use every available channel:** peer-matched platforms (Pramp, Interviewing.io) for realistic pressure with a stranger, and lower-stakes tools (even talking through a design out loud to an AI assistant) for repetition between scheduled mocks.

## Practice tip

After every mock (peer, recorded, or otherwise), write down the single biggest thing to fix next time — not a general "do better," but something concrete ("I didn't ask about read/write ratio before designing," "I went silent for 90 seconds while coding"). That specific, one-item retro is what actually compounds across repeated mocks instead of just repeating the same gaps.
