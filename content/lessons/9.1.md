# Behavioral

Technical skill gets you the interview slot; behavioral answers are what convince someone you're safe to hire and work alongside. This unit turns real achievements you already have into a reusable story bank.

## The STAR method

Structure every behavioral answer as:

- **Situation** — brief context (one or two sentences, don't over-explain the setup).
- **Task** — what you specifically needed to accomplish, and why it mattered.
- **Action** — what *you* did, specifically. This is the part to spend the most time on — say "I decided to..." not "we decided to," even in team contexts, because the interviewer is evaluating your individual judgment.
- **Result** — the measurable outcome, ideally quantified, plus what you learned or would do differently.

## Building a story bank from what you already have

You don't need manufactured stories — you have real, defensible material:

- **The Redux/Context refactor (30%+ maintainability gain)** → a technical decision-making and code-quality-ownership story. Situation: legacy state management causing X problem. Task: improve maintainability without a full rewrite. Action: specific architectural choices you made and why. Result: the measured 30%+ improvement, and what you'd refine further.
- **HubSpot/Webflow/ZeroBounce integration (25% efficiency gain)** → a cross-functional collaboration and ambiguity story. This is the one to reach for when asked "tell me about a time you had to work with unclear requirements" or "a time you integrated with a system you didn't control."
- **The Physynex retrieval pipeline's original direction** → an initiative and product-thinking story: translating a real user need (patient safety, lead quality) into concrete AI feature requirements, before you'd even built the implementation yourself. This is a legitimately strong "ownership beyond your assigned scope" story.
- **The Month 3 rebuild and Month 4 agent work from this roadmap itself** → a self-directed learning and growth-mindset story: deliberately identifying your own gap (hands-on RAG implementation) and closing it methodically, rather than waiting for a job to force the issue.
- **Physynex and Croire Paints as shipped products, not just codebases** → entrepreneurial range and end-to-end ownership, genuinely unusual at 2–3 years of experience — most candidates this early only have "features I built inside someone else's product."

## Why "I" language matters even in team stories

Interviewers are evaluating **your** individual contribution and judgment, not your team's. A story that's entirely "we did X" gives an interviewer nothing to assess about you specifically — always identify the decisions, trade-offs, or actions that were specifically yours, even while acknowledging it was a team effort.

## Preparing, not memorizing

Write bullet-point notes for each story (situation/task/action/result, 3-5 bullets each), not a word-for-word script. A memorized script sounds rehearsed and breaks down under a follow-up question that doesn't match your script; bullet points let you speak naturally while hitting the structure.

## Practice tip

For each story above, prepare it for at least two different common questions (e.g. the Redux story can answer both "tell me about a technical decision you're proud of" and "tell me about a time you improved something without being asked") — most real behavioral questions are variations on a small number of underlying themes, so a handful of well-prepared stories covers far more ground than it first appears.
